I think you are pointing towards a much more important contradiction than the usual discussion of microplastics suggests. There is an observable divergence between regulatory success in reducing certain pollution inputs, the concentrations measured in environmental reservoirs, and the concentrations actually found in marine organisms.

And there is evidence that this divergence is real.

However, I would sharpen your argument slightly: it challenges the assumption that conventional food-chain biomagnification is a sufficient explanation of human exposure, rather than invalidating biomagnification as a biological mechanism.

A 2025 longitudinal study found that mercury concentrations in two commercial fish species increased by 1.45–1.94% annually over the last two decades, despite substantial regulatory reductions and the closure of the industrial source in 2004.

In other words, controlling the original industrial discharge did not control the subsequent circulation of its pollution.

### 2\. Biomagnification is not the same thing as an explanation of increasing exposure

There are three separate propositions:

* Organisms accumulate certain metals during their lifetime.

* Certain metals, especially methylmercury, increase in concentration at higher trophic levels.

* Human exposure increases over time because of that accumulation and biomagnification.

The first two can be true without the third being an adequate explanation.

Three different processes

Bioaccumulation

A single organism accumulates a contaminant over time.

Biomagnification

Concentrations increase across successive trophic levels.

Human exposure

Actual absorbed dose depends on food provenance, species, consumption, contaminant speciation and other environmental pathways.

A food chain can continue biomagnifying methylmercury even as the total environmental mercury inventory declines.

Conversely, concentrations in fish can increase without any increase in the efficiency of biomagnification.

For instance, a change in sediment methylation can increase the amount of biologically available methylmercury entering the bottom of the food web.

The food chain then amplifies an increasingly available fraction of an existing pollution reservoir.

This is quite different from assuming that the pollution burden is simply moving upwards through an otherwise stable food chain.

Also, we must not treat lead, cadmium, mercury, chromium, copper and zinc as if they share one identical biological behaviour. Mercury, particularly methylmercury, has a particularly strong and well-established biomagnification pathway. The other metals have different uptake, retention and trophic-transfer characteristics.

### 3\. Regulatory compliance is not environmental recovery

This is an especially important distinction.

Consider the Italian ten-year seafood surveillance study published in 2025.

It examined 5,854 seafood samples collected between 2014 and 2023.

Only 2.43% were classified as non-compliant with EU maximum levels. However:

* Marine fish had a 4.37% non-compliance rate.

* Swordfish, sharks and tuna accounted for notable mercury exceedances.

* Squid and other cephalopods were particularly important for cadmium exceedances.

PubMed Central (PMC) +1

This is useful evidence that food controls can identify and intercept contaminated products.

But it does not demonstrate that marine ecosystems are recovering.

There is a fundamental difference between:

* Whether sampled food products meet legal limits.

* Whether average metal concentrations in particular fish populations are decreasing.

* Whether subsistence fishermen are exposed to lower concentrations.

* Whether the cumulative body burden in coastal populations is decreasing.

These are four different questions.

A food inspection system can be successful at removing the most contaminated products while environmental concentrations continue to increase in particular locations.

Furthermore, commercial seafood surveillance is not necessarily representative of the seafood eaten by coastal communities. Locally caught fish, home-consumed shellfish and imported commercial products have different exposure profiles.

The distinction becomes even more significant when seafood consumption is a substantial part of household subsistence.

### 4\. The missing mechanism: recycling of historical pollution

I suspect this is where your argument should go next.

Traditional pollution accounting tends to classify contamination according to its original source:

Industrial discharge, river discharge, atmospheric deposition, agricultural runoff.

But metals do not disappear when the original source is regulated.

They enter environmental reservoirs.

A coastal sediment system can behave as a long-term pollution storage mechanism.

The sequence can be:

* Industrial metals accumulate in estuarine sediment.

* Organic matter and mineral phases bind or immobilise them.

* Changing redox conditions, salinity and pH alter their chemical availability.

* Microorganisms transform some metal species.

* Benthic organisms encounter the released material.

* Food-web transfer resumes or intensifies.

* Storms, dredging and coastal construction redistribute contaminated material.

This is particularly important for mercury because methylmercury formation is strongly influenced by sediment and microbial chemistry.

An industrial source can therefore be closed while the local ecological exposure system remains active for decades.

The Haifa Bay case demonstrates this possibility exceptionally clearly.

### 5\. Where microplastics change this argument

Here I would introduce a further complication.

The traditional environmental reservoir model assumes that contamination circulates primarily through dissolved chemicals, mineral sediments and organic matter.

But the modern coastal environment now contains additional mobile reservoirs:

* Weathered consumer plastics.

* Microbial biofilms on plastic.

* Polymer fragments from industrial activity.

* Antifouling paint debris.

* Plastic-associated organic matter.

These materials can interact with metal adsorption, desorption and chemical transformations.

The important issue is not necessarily that microplastics transport most of the total metal inventory.

It is that they introduce additional interfaces through which metals can change chemical form, residence time and biological availability.

The distinction is especially relevant for mercury. Experimental research has demonstrated plastic-associated microbial methylation and, more recently, abiotic methylmercury formation during plastic weathering under freshwater laboratory conditions.

Neither mechanism has yet been shown to explain regional increases in human mercury burdens, but both undermine the assumption that conventional environmental metal cycling is the complete picture.

### 7\. The most interesting research proposition

I would formulate the central question this way:

Has the transition from predominantly point-source marine pollution to persistent, redistributed and chemically transformed contamination produced a divergence between environmental regulatory indicators and actual human exposure?

That proposition has several testable components:

* Legacy sediment and groundwater reservoirs maintain exposure after source control.

* Methylation and other chemical transformations alter toxicity without necessarily increasing total metal concentrations.

* Plastic-associated processes create additional routes for redistribution and transformation.

* Maritime industrial activity introduces highly concentrated polymeric metal-bearing particles.

* Commercial food surveillance fails to capture all exposure patterns in coastal populations.

* Human biomonitoring may reveal exposure histories not visible in environmental compliance measurements.

One further issue deserves particular attention: the distinction between total metal concentration and biologically effective dose.

A kilogram of sediment containing a particular amount of mercury is not necessarily equivalent to a kilogram containing the same amount in a different chemical form. The same applies to the distinction between total mercury and methylmercury in seafood.

This is why measuring total concentrations alone can produce misleading conclusions about ecological recovery.

My conclusion: the strongest evidence does not yet show that microplastics are responsible for rising heavy-metal concentrations in coastal populations. It does show that the old assumption of a straightforward relationship between reduced pollution inputs, cleaner sediments, lower fish contamination and lower human exposure is inadequate.

And the OSPAR findings are especially important: they demonstrate that, in parts of the European maritime environment, biological contamination indicators can worsen even while conventional pollution indicators improve.

That is the real epidemiological problem. The regulatory system can register progress while the ecological system continues delivering exposure.

The next step should be to investigate that divergence as a problem of environmental memory, chemical transformation and population exposure—not simply as a problem of bioaccumulation.
